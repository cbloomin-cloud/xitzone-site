Program the room.

By Caydrick · 4 min read

Most training programs are written as if every gym is the same. Then they meet the real world: a fire station with a sled and two kettlebells, an apartment fitness room the size of a closet, a high school weight room with forty athletes and twelve racks.

I spend my days designing those spaces. The longer I do it, the more convinced I am of one thing: the best program starts with the floor plan.

The room sets the rules

Every facility quietly decides how people will train in it. The equipment, the flow, the square footage, how many people show up at once and how long they can stay. A great program works with those rules instead of fighting them.

Design the space for the training. Then design the training for the space.

What that looks like

  • Hybrid and CrossFit gyms have turf, sleds, ergs and open floor, so programs should use all of it: running, stations and carries. That's Hybrid Engine.
  • Commercial clubs are built on machines, cables and dumbbell rows. Use them to build muscle safely and simply with Foundation Strength.
  • Corporate wellness centers live on 30-minute windows and mixed fitness levels. Clock-based formats like EMOMs and Tabatas let everyone train together: EMOM + Tabata 30.
  • K-12 and Higher Ed weight rooms move big groups through racks and platforms. Tempo-based triphasic work keeps athletes safe and builds real power: Triphasic Athlete.
  • Municipal fire and police facilities need strength and work capacity that carry over to the job: sleds, sandbags, stairs, rucks. That's Duty Ready.
  • Multifamily and hospitality rooms are compact: dumbbells, a bench, body weight. Home Base is built for exactly that.

The questions I ask first

Before I sketch a single rack, I want to know who trains here, when they show up, how long they have, and what success looks like for them. The answers shape the space, and they should shape the program too.

That's why every free program on this site starts with one question: where do you train?
